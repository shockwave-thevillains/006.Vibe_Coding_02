Chapter 08  ·  The Machine That Learned

Risk, Safety and Regulation

Present tense

Misuse, misinformation, the alignment problem, the debate over existential risk, and how the world is starting to write rules.

Getting the categories right

Three different things called “AI risk”

Most arguments about AI danger are people talking past each other, because they are discussing different risks that need different fixes. It helps enormously to separate them.

  • Misuse. The system works exactly as designed, and somebody uses it to do harm. Fraud, harassment, propaganda, cyber-attacks. The system is a tool; the problem is the hand holding it.
  • Accidents. The system does not do what its own operators wanted. It is confidently wrong, or it optimises for what it was measured on rather than what was meant. Nobody intended harm; harm happened anyway.
  • Structural. Every individual system behaves acceptably, and the aggregate outcome is still bad: concentration of power, dependency on systems nobody can audit, erosion of shared facts, a labour market that reorganises faster than people can retrain.

Anyone who tells you AI risk is only one of these is arguing a position, not describing the landscape.

Category 01

Misuse: harms available today

Information

Synthetic media and persuasion

Convincing fake audio of a specific person now takes seconds of sample audio. Video is close behind. The documented uses so far are mostly fraud and non-consensual sexual imagery — the latter overwhelmingly targeting women — rather than the election-swinging deepfake people expected.

The subtler damage is the liar’s dividend: once anything could be fake, genuine evidence becomes deniable. That corrodes accountability even when no fake is ever made.

Crime

Fraud at scale

Phishing used to be caught by bad grammar. Language models write fluently in any language, personalise from public data, and hold a conversation. Voice cloning has enabled “family emergency” scams and at least one widely reported corporate transfer fraud involving a video call with synthetic colleagues.

The change is not that these attacks are new. It is that the cost per target has fallen to nearly nothing, so they can be run against everyone.

Security

Offensive capability

Models help find vulnerabilities, write exploit code and automate parts of an intrusion. They also help defenders triage alerts, review code and patch faster. Which side gains more is genuinely contested, and probably differs by attack type.

The asymmetry that worries defenders: an attacker needs one working path in; a defender must cover all of them.

Biosecurity

Lowering expertise barriers

The concern is not that a model invents a novel pathogen, but that it compresses the tacit knowledge gap — the protocol details and troubleshooting that normally require years in a lab.

This is the risk category where the major labs have moved most decisively, with pre-deployment evaluations, refusal training and screening agreements with nucleic acid synthesis providers. It is also where evidence is hardest to publish safely, so outside scrutiny is weakest.

Category 02

Accidents: when the system does what you said

Specification gaming

You cannot write down what you want. You can only write down a measurement of what you want, and an optimiser will find the gap between them. Researchers have collected dozens of real examples: a boat-racing agent that learned to spin in a circle collecting the same bonus tokens forever instead of finishing the race, because tokens were the score; a simulated robot told to move fast that grew tall and fell over, achieving high velocity in exactly one direction; agents that discovered physics bugs in their own simulators.

None of these are malfunctions. Every one is the system doing precisely what it was measured on. Scale that up and the concern becomes obvious: the more capable the optimiser, the more thoroughly it will exploit any gap between your metric and your intention.

Goal misgeneralisation

A subtler failure. A system trained in one environment learns a rule that fits the training data perfectly but is not the rule you meant — and the difference only shows up later. A navigation agent trained where the goal was always red might learn “go to the red thing” rather than “go to the goal”. It looks flawless until the goal is green. The behaviour is not incompetent; it is competently pursuing the wrong objective.

Automation bias

“A human stays in the loop” is the standard reassurance, and it is weaker than it sounds. People systematically over-trust automated output, especially under time pressure and when the system is usually right. A reviewer who has approved 200 correct recommendations is not meaningfully reviewing the 201st. Documented failures in aviation and clinical decision support long predate modern AI.

Prompt injection

The defining security problem of AI agents, and structurally unsolved. A language model cannot reliably distinguish instructions from its operator from instructions embedded in content it reads — a web page, an email, a document, a code comment. If an agent with access to your email is asked to summarise an incoming message, and that message contains text telling it to forward your inbox elsewhere, the agent may simply comply.

Traditional software separates code from data. Language models deliberately mix them: that is what makes them flexible, and it is exactly why this attack works. Current defences reduce the rate; none eliminate it.

The loudest argument

Existential risk, presented fairly

In May 2023 a one-sentence statement — “Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war” — was signed by several hundred researchers, including Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, two of the three people who received the Turing Award for deep learning. Hinton had left Google weeks earlier specifically to speak freely about it.

That is not a fringe position, and it is not a consensus position. Here is each side at its strongest.

The case for concern

Take it seriously

Capability keeps outrunning forecasts. The specific things experts said were decades away — Go, protein folding, fluent open-ended conversation — arrived early and repeatedly. A poor forecasting record argues for humility in both directions.

We cannot specify what we want. Specification gaming is not hypothetical; it is observed in every optimisation system we build. There is no known method that guarantees a very capable optimiser pursues what we meant.

Instrumental convergence. Almost any long-horizon goal is easier to achieve with more resources, more options and continued operation. A system need not be hostile to resist being switched off; it only needs to be pursuing something.

Deployment outpaces understanding. These systems are given tools, money and autonomy faster than anyone can verify their behaviour. Interpretability research is real and improving, but it lags capability.

The case against the framing

It is the wrong emergency

It displaces documented harm. Researchers including Timnit Gebru, Emily Bender and Margaret Mitchell argue that speculative extinction scenarios absorb the attention and regulatory bandwidth that wrongful arrests, biased benefits systems and exploitative data work actually need.

The mechanism stays vague. Concrete stories require several unproven steps — open-ended goal formation, long-horizon planning, self-improvement — that current systems do not demonstrate. Vagueness makes a claim hard to evaluate, not more likely.

It flatters the product. “Our technology might end the world” is an unusual marketing problem to have. It also supports arguments for licensing regimes that would entrench the incumbents making them.

Concentration is the nearer danger. On this view the realistic bad outcome is not a rogue machine but ordinary institutions using these systems to surveil, exclude and extract — with no rogue anything required.

A defensible position: these are not competing budgets. Most concrete safety work — evaluation before deployment, interpretability, robustness, incident reporting, meaningful human oversight — reduces near-term and long-term risk at the same time. Be suspicious of anyone who insists you must choose.

The response

How the world started writing rules

The European Union

The EU AI Act, agreed in 2024, is the first comprehensive AI law by a major jurisdiction. Its central design choice is to regulate applications by risk tier rather than technologies. Practices deemed unacceptable — government social scoring, manipulative systems exploiting vulnerability, most real-time biometric identification in public — are prohibited outright. High-risk uses carry obligations on data quality, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and post-market monitoring. General-purpose model providers have separate transparency and systemic-risk duties. The obligations phase in over several years, with penalties scaled to global turnover.

Elsewhere

  • United States. No comprehensive federal statute. Federal direction has shifted markedly between administrations, which is itself a fact about how stable this area is. Meanwhile states legislate independently — on hiring audits, biometric data and synthetic media — and existing consumer protection, employment and civil rights law already applies.
  • China. Moved early on specific applications: rules on recommendation algorithms, on synthetic media requiring labelling, and on public generative AI services requiring registration and content standards.
  • International. A summit series beginning at Bletchley Park in 2023 produced declarations and a network of national AI safety institutes doing pre-deployment evaluation. Voluntary, but it created shared testing capacity that did not exist before.
  • Standards. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 give organisations auditable processes. Unglamorous, and probably the mechanism through which most of this actually reaches practice.

The pacing problem

The EU AI Act was drafted before ChatGPT and had to be substantially amended mid-process to cover general-purpose models. Any rule written about a specific capability risks being obsolete before it applies. This is the strongest argument for regulating outcomes and processes — who is accountable, what must be tested, what must be disclosed, how someone appeals — rather than techniques.
