Chapter 07  ·  The Machine That Learned

Ethics: Fairness, Work and Ownership

Present tense

Bias in training data, the hidden labour behind AI, copyright fights, the environmental bill, and who gets a say.

Framing

Not science fiction. Things that already happened.

Ethics discussions about AI often jump straight to robot uprisings. That is a mistake, because it lets the actual harms — which are documented, dated and attributable — slip past unexamined.

Everything on this page has already occurred. Real people lost jobs, homes, custody of their children, and their liberty. In several cases the systems responsible were procured in good faith by competent organisations who believed they were removing human bias, not automating it.

This chapter is about harms happening now. Chapter 08 covers catastrophic and speculative risk. Both are worth taking seriously; conflating them helps nobody.

Problem 01

Bias is not a bug in the code

A machine learning system finds patterns in its training data and reproduces them. If the data records a world with discrimination in it, the system learns the discrimination — not as a mistake, but as its job, done correctly.

Where it enters

  • Historical data. Train a hiring model on ten years of a company’s decisions and it learns who that company hired, including whoever it wrongly passed over.
  • Sampling. If a face dataset is overwhelmingly light-skinned, the model is simply worse on everyone else, and nobody notices because average accuracy looks fine.
  • Proxy variables. Remove race from the inputs and the model finds it anyway, through postcode, school, name or shopping history. Removing the protected field hides the bias rather than fixing it.
  • Labelling. Somebody decided what counted as a “good employee” or a “risky” applicant. Their judgement becomes ground truth.
  • Feedback loops. Predict more crime in a neighbourhood → send more police → record more arrests there → the next model is even more confident. The system generates its own evidence.

Documented cases

  • COMPAS (2016). A risk score used in US courts. ProPublica’s analysis found Black defendants who did not reoffend were roughly twice as likely to have been labelled high risk as comparable white defendants. The vendor disputed the methodology, and statisticians showed the two sides were using incompatible definitions of fairness — which, it turns out, cannot all be satisfied at once.
  • Amazon’s hiring tool (2018). Trained on a decade of CVs from a male-dominated industry, it penalised the word “women’s” and downgraded graduates of two women’s colleges. Amazon scrapped it.
  • Gender Shades (2018). Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested three commercial gender-classification systems. Error rates for lighter-skinned men were under 1%. For darker-skinned women they ran as high as roughly 35%. The systems had shipped; nobody had measured this.
  • Wrongful arrests. Multiple people in the United States — Robert Williams in Detroit in 2020 among the first publicly reported — have been arrested on the basis of a face recognition match that was simply wrong. The known cases disproportionately involve Black men.

The trap in “just make it fair”

There are several reasonable mathematical definitions of fairness — equal false positive rates, equal accuracy across groups, scores that mean the same thing for everyone. It has been proved that when base rates differ between groups, you generally cannot satisfy all of them simultaneously. Choosing which to satisfy is a political and moral decision that no amount of engineering can make for you.

Problem 02

The people who make AI work, and are not in the photos

“The model learned it from data” obscures a great deal of human work. Somebody drew the boxes around every pedestrian in the self-driving training set. Somebody decided which of two chatbot answers was better, thousands of times, so that RLHF had preferences to learn from. Somebody watched the violent and abusive material so the safety filters could be tuned.

That work is real employment, and a lot of it is outsourced to Kenya, the Philippines, India and Venezuela, paid by the task, with limited job security. Reporting in 2023 described workers in Nairobi labelling graphic material for content filters at rates around $1.32 to $2 an hour, several of whom described lasting psychological harm. The same broad pattern — piece rates, no mental health provision, non-disclosure agreements — recurs across the industry.

Content moderation for social platforms has produced similar outcomes for longer, including a $52 million settlement in 2020 for US moderators who developed PTSD. AI has not removed this labour; it has increased demand for it while making it less visible.

Why it matters ethically: the cost of building these systems is not only paid in electricity and chips. Part of it is paid by people who are not in the launch announcements, do not hold equity, and cannot say what they worked on.

Problem 03

Who owns the training data?

Frontier models are trained on text and images scraped from the open web at a scale where individual permission was never sought. Whether that is lawful is being decided in courts right now, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, and the answers are not yet consistent.

  • The New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft (2023). Alleges large-scale copying of articles for training and reproduction of passages in output.
  • Getty Images v. Stability AI. Getty pointed to outputs carrying distorted remnants of its watermark as evidence of what was in the training set.
  • Authors’ and artists’ class actions. Multiple suits over books and artworks used without licence or payment.

The legal question is roughly whether training is transformative fair use or large-scale reproduction. The economic question is sharper: these systems compete with the people whose work trained them — illustrators, stock photographers, copywriters, translators, voice actors — using capabilities derived from that work.

Responses so far include licensing deals between labs and publishers, opt-out signals of contested effectiveness, provenance standards for generated media, and consent-first datasets. None is yet the norm.

Problem 04 & 05

The bill, and who gets to send it

Environment

Electricity, water, hardware

Training a frontier model consumes energy on the scale of hundreds of homes for a year. Serving it to millions of users afterwards consumes considerably more in total — inference, not training, is now the larger share.

Data centres also consume fresh water for cooling, sometimes in regions already under water stress, and the chips depend on mined materials and a highly concentrated manufacturing supply chain.

Reporting is patchy and often voluntary. The honest position is that the footprint is significant, growing quickly, and harder to audit than it should be — while the same technology also has genuine applications in grid optimisation and materials science. Both can be true; neither cancels the other.

Power

A very short list of builders

Training a frontier model costs on the order of tens to hundreds of millions of dollars in compute alone, plus data, plus talent. That confines the frontier to a handful of companies and states.

The consequences are structural. Academic labs cannot replicate frontier results, so independent verification weakens. Safety research increasingly happens inside the organisations with the strongest commercial reasons to ship. The values baked into refusals, tone and defaults are chosen by small teams and applied to hundreds of millions of people.

Open-weight models push back on this — anyone can inspect, fine-tune and run them — at the cost of removing the ability to withdraw a model once released. That trade-off has no clean answer, and it is the live argument in Chapter 08.

Problem 06

Automated decisions about people who cannot appeal

The clearest harms come not from the most advanced systems but from ordinary ones, deployed at scale against people with no practical way to contest the result.

The Dutch childcare benefits scandal

Between roughly 2013 and 2019 the Dutch tax authority used a risk-scoring system to flag possible childcare benefit fraud. Having a second nationality was among the factors that raised suspicion. Tens of thousands of families — disproportionately those with migrant backgrounds — were wrongly accused, ordered to repay sums many did not owe, and driven into debt. Over a thousand children were placed in foster care. The Dutch government resigned over it in January 2021.

Robodebt

Australia’s automated welfare debt recovery scheme, running from 2016, calculated alleged overpayments by averaging annual income across fortnights — a method that systematically invented debts for anyone with irregular work. Around 470,000 wrongly issued debts were later cancelled or refunded. A Royal Commission in 2023 found the scheme unlawful and described it as a “crude and cruel mechanism”. It was linked to multiple suicides.

Neither used deep learning. Both illustrate the pattern that matters: an automated decision, applied to a large population, with the burden of proof reversed and no accessible route to challenge it. Adding a more capable model to that structure makes it faster, not fairer.

What actually helps

Not slogans. Concrete, boring practices: documentation (model cards and dataset datasheets stating what a system was built for, tested on, and known to fail at); disaggregated evaluation (accuracy reported per group, not averaged); a human who can actually overrule the system and is not punished for doing so; a working appeals route with the burden of proof in the right place; independent audit with real access; and the option not to build it, which remains available at every stage and is used far too rarely.
