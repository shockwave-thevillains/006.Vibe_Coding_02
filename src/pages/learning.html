The reversal

Stop writing rules. Start counting.

The most important change in the history of AI is not a machine or a person. It is a reversal of direction, and it happened quietly, mostly during the second winter, mostly without anyone calling it artificial intelligence.

The old way: a human works out the rules, writes them down, and the computer applies them. The new way: the human supplies examples with the right answers attached, and the computer works out the rules itself.

Consider spam filtering. The symbolic approach is to write rules: block anything containing “FREE MONEY”. Spammers write “FRΞΞ M0NEY”, and you write another rule, forever. The statistical approach is to take 50,000 emails already labelled spam or not-spam, count how often each word appears in each pile, and let the machine work out that certain words shift the odds. Nobody writes a rule. When spammers adapt, you retrain on new mail and the filter adapts with them.

This works. It works so well that by the mid-1990s it had taken over speech recognition, translation, search ranking, credit scoring, fraud detection and recommendation — while the term “AI” was still radioactive.

1986

Backpropagation: how a network learns

The obstacle Perceptrons identified in 1969 was not that multi-layer networks were too weak. It was that nobody knew how to train them. If the final answer is wrong, and the error passed through three hidden layers of thousands of connections on its way out, which connections do you blame?

In 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published a paper in Nature that made the answer practical. (The mathematics had appeared earlier — Seppo Linnainmaa in 1970, Paul Werbos in 1974 — but this is the paper that convinced the field.)

The idea, without the calculus

  1. Guess. Push an example through the network with whatever weights it currently has. Out comes an answer — at first, garbage.
  2. Measure. Compare the answer to the correct one. The gap is the loss — a single number saying how wrong you were.
  3. Assign blame backwards. Starting at the output and working back layer by layer, calculate how much each weight contributed to that error. This is just the chain rule from calculus, applied mechanically.
  4. Nudge. Move every weight a small step in the direction that would have reduced the error.
  5. Repeat a few million times.

That is it. No understanding, no insight, no rules — just an enormous number of tiny corrections, each one making the network slightly less wrong. Every neural network in existence, including the one behind whatever chatbot you last used, is trained by this procedure. Only the scale has changed.

Why it took twenty-five more years to matter

Backpropagation worked in 1986 — on small networks, small datasets and the computers of 1986. Deep networks trained on those machines were slow, unstable and no better than simpler methods. The algorithm was not the bottleneck. Data and hardware were, and neither arrived until the late 2000s.

1988 – 2001

The toolbox that ran the world before deep learning

Neural networks were only one branch. Several others worked better at the time, and several are still the right choice today.

1988 — Judea Pearl

Bayesian networks

A way to represent uncertain knowledge as a graph of causes and effects, and to update all your beliefs correctly when a new fact arrives. Pearl gave AI a principled language for reasoning under uncertainty — something logic could not do — and later, a mathematics of causation that lets you distinguish “these two things occur together” from “this one causes that one”. He received the Turing Award in 2011.

1989 — Yann LeCun

Convolutional networks

LeCun’s LeNet read handwritten digits by exploiting a fact about images: a shape means the same thing wherever it appears. So instead of learning every position separately, the network learns a small set of feature detectors and slides them across the whole image. By the late 1990s, systems built on this were reading a large share of the cheques processed in the United States. This architecture would win everything in 2012.

1995 — Cortes & Vapnik

Support vector machines

A method that finds the dividing line with the widest possible margin between two classes, and — through the “kernel trick” — can draw curved boundaries by mapping the data into a higher-dimensional space without ever computing the coordinates. Elegant, mathematically well-understood, and effective on modest datasets. SVMs beat neural networks on most benchmarks for over a decade.

1995 – 2001 — ensembles

Boosting and random forests

Instead of one clever model, train hundreds of mediocre ones and combine their votes. AdaBoost trains each new model to focus on the examples the previous ones got wrong. Random forests grow many decision trees on random subsets of the data. Both are fast, resistant to overfitting, and need little tuning — which is why they remain the default for tabular business data to this day.

1990s — IBM and others

Statistical language processing

IBM’s translation researchers threw away the grammar rules and used aligned bilingual texts — famously the bilingual transcripts of the Canadian parliament — to compute which phrases tend to correspond. Frederick Jelinek’s remark that “every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recogniser goes up” became the era’s unofficial motto.

1997 — Hochreiter & Schmidhuber

LSTM

Ordinary recurrent networks forgot: over a long sequence, the error signal shrank toward zero before it could reach the early steps. The Long Short-Term Memory cell added explicit gates that decide what to keep, what to discard and what to output. It was largely ignored for a decade, then powered Google Translate and speech recognition from roughly 2014 until transformers replaced it.

The public milestones

Machines beat humans, on television

1997 — Deep Blue defeats Garry Kasparov

IBM’s chess machine beat the reigning world champion 3½–2½ in a six-game match. It was a landmark, and it is worth being precise about what kind.

Deep Blue did not learn. It was custom hardware — 480 dedicated chess chips — evaluating around 200 million positions per second, guided by an evaluation function hand-tuned with grandmaster help. It was the symbolic tradition, perfected and given enormous horsepower: search plus human-supplied knowledge.

The lesson people drew was double-edged. Chess had been the canonical test of intelligence since the 1950s; a machine won it without anything resembling understanding. That suggested either that chess was never a good test, or that “understanding” is less necessary than we assumed. The argument continues.

2011 — Watson wins Jeopardy!

IBM’s Watson beat champions Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter at a quiz game built on puns, wordplay and general knowledge. Unlike Deep Blue, Watson was fundamentally statistical: it ran hundreds of scoring algorithms over 200 million pages of text in parallel and combined their confidence scores, answering only when the combined confidence cleared a threshold.

Watson’s subsequent decade — heavily marketed for medicine, quietly scaled back — became a cautionary tale about the distance between a demo and a deployment.

2002

Roomba

The first genuinely mass-market robot — millions sold. Its intelligence was minimal by design, which was exactly why it worked commercially when far cleverer robots did not.

2004 – 2005

The DARPA Grand Challenge

In 2004 no self-driving vehicle finished the desert course; the best managed 11 km of 240. In 2005 five vehicles finished, led by Stanford’s Stanley, which used machine learning to interpret its laser scans. The entire autonomous-vehicle industry traces to those two races.

2006

Hinton renames the field

A paper on layer-by-layer pre-training of “deep belief networks” showed that deep networks could be trained after all. Hinton deliberately used the term deep learning — partly to signal a real advance, partly because “neural networks” still made reviewers hostile.

2009

ImageNet

Fei-Fei Li’s team published a labelled dataset of over 14 million images across 22,000 categories, built with crowdsourced human labelling. It was an unfashionable project — the field valued algorithms, not datasets. It turned out to be the missing ingredient.

2011

Siri ships

Speech recognition, previously a research embarrassment, arrived in a phone in everyone’s pocket — built on statistical acoustic models rather than linguistic rules.

Three things arrived at once

By around 2010, everything needed for the next chapter was in place — and none of it was a new idea. All three were quantities.

  • Data. The web produced labelled examples at a scale nobody could have assembled deliberately: billions of captioned photographs, tagged videos, translated documents, product reviews with star ratings. ImageNet turned some of it into a benchmark.
  • Compute. Graphics cards, built to render game worlds, turned out to be almost perfectly suited to the matrix multiplications that neural networks are made of — thousands of simple operations in parallel. A GPU could train a network tens of times faster than a CPU, for the price of a games console.
  • Algorithms that were already waiting. Backpropagation from 1986, convolutional networks from 1989, LSTM from 1997. Nothing new was needed. They just had to be run at a scale their inventors could not afford.

The uncomfortable pattern. The single biggest driver of progress in AI has not been better ideas. It has been more compute applied to old ideas. Richard Sutton named this the bitter lesson: over seventy years, general methods that scale with computation have consistently beaten methods that encode human insight — and researchers have consistently found this unwelcome.
