1936

A machine made of paper

Artificial intelligence begins before computers existed — with a 24-year-old mathematician trying to settle an argument about the limits of mathematics.

In 1936, Alan Turing imagined a device so simple it sounds useless: an endless paper tape divided into squares, a head that can read one square at a time, and a small table of instructions telling it what to write and which way to move. That is the whole machine. No screen, no keyboard, no memory chips.

Turing then proved something startling. Any calculation that a human being can carry out by following a fixed procedure — long division, sorting a list, checking a proof — can be carried out by that tape-and-head device. And a single “universal” version of it, given a description of any other such machine, can imitate it exactly.

That is the idea behind every computer you have ever used: one piece of hardware that becomes any program you feed it. It is also the first serious reason to suspect that thinking might be something a machine could do, if thinking turns out to be a procedure.

Why this matters for AI

If mental work is computation, then a universal machine can in principle do it. The entire field rests on that “if”. Seventy years of argument have not settled whether it is true — only how far you can get by assuming it.

1943 – 1949

Three ideas arrive at once

Between the war and the first computers, four separate research communities each produced a piece of the puzzle. None of them called it artificial intelligence.

YearIdeaWhoWhat it contributed
1943 The artificial neuron Warren McCulloch, Walter Pitts A brain cell modelled as a tiny logic gate: add up the incoming signals, and fire only if the total crosses a threshold. Networks of these, they showed, can compute any logical function. Every neural network alive today is a descendant of this one-page idea.
1948 Information theory Claude Shannon Proved that information is a measurable quantity, gave it a unit (the bit), and showed how much of it a noisy channel can carry. It turned messages, images and text into the same kind of thing: numbers a machine can process.
1948 Cybernetics Norbert Wiener Studied feedback: systems that sense their own output and correct course — a thermostat, a gun turret, an animal reaching for food. It made “goal-directed behaviour” something you could engineer rather than something only living things had.
1949 A rule for learning Donald Hebb Proposed that when two neurons fire together repeatedly, the connection between them strengthens — usually shortened to “cells that fire together, wire together”. It gave a physical account of how experience could change a network. Learning became adjustable connection strengths.

1950

The question, and the trick for answering it

In October 1950 Turing published Computing Machinery and Intelligence in the philosophy journal Mind. It opens with the sentence “I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’” — and then, in the next paragraph, throws that question away.

His reason was practical. “Think” and “machine” are words whose ordinary meanings would have to be settled by opinion poll, and a poll is not science. So he replaced the question with a game.

May not machines carry out something which ought to be described as thinking but which is very different from what a man does?

Alan Turing, 1950

The imitation game

A human judge sits at a terminal and holds two text conversations at once: one with a person, one with a machine. The judge cannot see or hear either. Their job is to work out which is which. If the judge cannot do better than chance, Turing argued, we have no principled reason left to deny the machine the word “thinking”.

The design is deliberately cruel to the machine and generous to the human. Text only, so no advantage from a body or a voice. Any topic at all, so no narrow domain to hide in. The judge is actively trying to catch it out.

The objections he answered in advance

Most of the paper is Turing pre-emptively demolishing nine objections. They are still the objections people raise today:

  • The theological objection — thinking requires a soul. Turing answers, drily, that this places limits on God’s power to grant one.
  • “Machines can only do what we tell them.” Turing points out that he is routinely surprised by his own programs, and that a machine which learns will do things its programmer never specified.
  • Lady Lovelace’s objection — machines cannot originate anything. He replies that neither, arguably, do people: we recombine what we were taught.
  • The argument from consciousness — the machine does not feel anything. Turing notes that the only way to be sure any person feels anything is to be that person, and we do not normally let this stop us.

He also made a prediction: by the year 2000, a machine would fool an average judge 30% of the time in a five-minute conversation. He was roughly two decades early — but he was right that it would happen, and right about which approach would get there (“child machines” that learn, rather than adults programmed with rules).

A cost worth naming. In 1952 Turing was prosecuted for homosexuality, then a crime in Britain, and sentenced to chemical castration. He lost his security clearance. He died in 1954, aged 41, from cyanide poisoning — ruled a suicide. He never saw a single one of his predictions tested. The British government apologised in 2009 and granted a posthumous pardon in 2013.

Summer 1956

Dartmouth: a field gets a name

In 1955 a young mathematician named John McCarthy wrote a funding proposal to the Rockefeller Foundation with Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester of IBM and Claude Shannon. They asked for money to bring ten people to Dartmouth College for eight weeks. The proposal contains what is probably the most confident sentence in the history of computing:

The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.

Dartmouth proposal, 31 August 1955

McCarthy needed a label for the topic. “Cybernetics” was taken and came with Wiener attached. “Automata studies” sounded dull and had not attracted the papers he wanted. So he coined artificial intelligence — partly, he later admitted, because it was vague enough that nobody could object that their work did not fit.

The workshop itself was a partial failure as an event. People arrived and left at different times, there was no fixed agenda, and no consensus emerged. But the group that passed through that summer — McCarthy, Minsky, Newell, Simon, Shannon, Samuel, Solomonoff — went on to run essentially every major AI laboratory in the United States for the next twenty years.

1950

Shannon programs chess

Shannon publishes the first serious paper on computer chess, introducing the idea of scoring a board position and searching ahead a fixed number of moves.

1951

SNARC, the first neural machine

Minsky and Dean Edmonds build a 40-neuron machine out of vacuum tubes and surplus bomber parts. It learns to run a maze by strengthening connections that worked.

1955

Logic Theorist

Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw write a program that proves theorems from Principia Mathematica — and finds a proof shorter and more elegant than the book’s. The journal editor declined to publish it with a computer as co-author.

1956

The Dartmouth workshop

Ten researchers, eight weeks, one new name. Logic Theorist is demonstrated. Nothing is concluded — but everyone leaves believing the problem is tractable.

1957

The perceptron

Frank Rosenblatt builds a machine that learns to classify images by adjusting its own weights. The New York Times reports it is the embryo of a computer that will walk, talk, see and reproduce itself.

What the founders got right and wrong

Right: that intelligence could be studied as an engineering problem rather than a philosophical one. That search, learning and representation would be the core technical questions — they still are. That machines would eventually hold a fluent conversation.

Wrong: the timeline, by a factor of roughly ten. And, more deeply, the assumption that the hard part of intelligence is the part that feels hard to us. Chess and theorem-proving fell early. Recognising a face, picking up a cup, understanding a sentence in context — things a four-year-old does without effort — took another fifty years. This became known as Moravec’s paradox: the tasks evolution spent millions of years perfecting are the ones we find hardest to reproduce, precisely because they feel effortless and we have no idea how we do them.

That misjudgement set up everything in the next chapter: a decade of spectacular early results, followed by a wall.
