30 September 2012

The result that ended the argument

Every year since 2010, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge had asked competing teams to do one thing: sort 1.2 million photographs into 1,000 categories. Not “dog” but “Border collie”. Not “mushroom” but the specific species. It is genuinely hard, and human experts get about 5% of them wrong.

Progress had been incremental. The winning error rate fell from 28.2% to 25.8% in two years, through careful hand-engineering of visual features — edge detectors, texture descriptors, colour histograms designed by researchers who had spent careers on the problem.

Then a team of three from the University of Toronto — Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton — entered a deep convolutional neural network trained on two consumer gaming graphics cards. It scored 15.3%. Second place, using the established methods, scored 26.2%.

A margin like that is not an improvement. It is a different category of result. By the following year, essentially every serious entry was a neural network, and the hand-engineered feature detectors that had defined computer vision were abandoned within about eighteen months.

What was actually new in AlexNet

Almost nothing, individually. Convolutional networks came from LeCun in 1989. Backpropagation from 1986. What AlexNet combined was scale (60 million parameters, trained on GPUs for a week), ReLU activations (a simpler non-linearity that made deep networks train far faster), dropout (randomly switching off neurons during training so the network cannot over-rely on any one path), and data augmentation (flipping and cropping images to multiply the training set). Engineering, not theory.

The mechanism

Why “deep” is the important word

The old approach asked human researchers to design the features: write code that detects edges, then code that combines edges into shapes, then a classifier on top. Each stage was a research paper.

A deep network does the same thing — but it discovers each stage from the data. Early layers, entirely on their own, converge on edge and colour detectors that look remarkably like the ones neuroscientists find in the first stages of the mammalian visual cortex. Middle layers combine those into textures and simple shapes. Later layers respond to wheels, faces, doorknobs.

This is representation learning, and it is the actual breakthrough. The machine is not just learning to answer the question; it is learning what to look at. Because early layers learn generic features, a network trained on ImageNet can be re-purposed for medical scans or satellite imagery with a fraction of the data — transfer learning, now standard practice everywhere.

What made the depth trainable

  • ReLU — replacing smooth S-shaped activation functions with a blunt “output the input if positive, otherwise zero”. It sounds crude. It stopped the error signal from shrinking to nothing on its way back through the layers.
  • Dropout — randomly disabling half the neurons on each training pass, forcing the network to build redundant routes rather than fragile ones.
  • Batch normalisation (2015) — rescaling the values flowing between layers so they stay in a well-behaved range, which made much deeper networks train reliably.
  • Residual connections (2015) — letting each layer learn a small correction to what came before, rather than a whole new transformation. This one change took networks from about 20 usable layers to 150 and beyond.

2013 – 2017

Five years, five directions

2013

word2vec: meaning as geometry

Tomáš Mikolov’s team at Google trained a simple network to predict a word from its neighbours, then threw away the network and kept the numbers it had learned for each word. Those numbers — embeddings — placed related words near each other in space, and encoded relationships as directions: the step from king to queen was roughly the same step as from man to woman.

It was the first widely convincing demonstration that a machine could capture something about meaning purely from co-occurrence statistics. It also inherited every bias in the text, which is how the field learned that lesson the hard way.

2014

GANs: two networks in a duel

Ian Goodfellow’s idea, reportedly worked out after a pub argument: train two networks against each other. A generator tries to produce fake images. A discriminator tries to spot the fakes. Each one’s success is the other’s failure, so both improve.

Within four years the generator was producing photorealistic faces of people who do not exist. GANs gave the world its first serious taste of synthetic media — and the word “deepfake”.

2014

Sequence to sequence

Translation was reframed: one network reads the whole source sentence and compresses it into a vector; a second network reads that vector and writes the target sentence. No grammar, no dictionaries, no alignment rules.

The catch was the bottleneck — one fixed-size vector had to hold an entire sentence, and long sentences degraded badly. The fix, attention, arrived in 2015 and turned out to be the whole future. See Chapter 06.

2015

Learning to play from pixels

DeepMind’s DQN learned to play 49 Atari games from nothing but the raw screen and the score, using the same network and the same settings for every game. On about half of them it exceeded a professional human tester. It had no idea what a paddle or an alien was — only which pixel patterns preceded points.

This was reinforcement learning arriving at scale: no labelled answers, just a reward signal and a great many attempts.

March 2016 — the one people remember

AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol

Go had been AI’s standing embarrassment. The board is 19×19; the number of legal positions exceeds the count of atoms in the observable universe. Deep Blue’s brute-force search is useless here, and evaluating who is winning in Go is notoriously subtle. Experts in 2015 estimated a decade or more before a machine could beat a top professional.

DeepMind combined three things: a network that proposed plausible moves, a network that judged positions, and Monte Carlo tree search to explore the promising lines. It trained first on human games, then by playing millions of games against itself. It beat Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players of his generation, 4–1, watched live by over 200 million people.

In game two came move 37 — a shoulder hit on the fifth line that commentators initially assumed was a bug. It was not. It was a move no human would play, and it won the game. Lee Sedol later said it made him reconsider what he thought he knew about Go. In game four he answered with move 78, a wedge so unlikely that AlphaGo had assigned it almost no probability; the machine collapsed and lost. It remains the only game he won, and one of the most closely studied moves in the history of the game.

A year later AlphaGo Zero dropped the human games entirely. Given only the rules, playing solely against itself, it surpassed the version that beat Lee Sedol after three days of training — and rediscovered, then discarded as inferior, several joseki that human players had used for centuries.

Keep the ledger honest. Every system in this chapter does exactly one thing. AlphaGo cannot play chess. DQN cannot describe the game it just played. AlexNet cannot answer a question about the photo it labelled. This is narrow AI, however superhuman. The next chapter is about the first architecture that started to blur that line.
