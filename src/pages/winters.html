What a winter is

When the money stops

An AI winter is not a period when the science fails. It is a period when the funding fails — when the gap between what was promised and what was delivered gets wide enough that the people paying stop paying, and everyone else looks away for a decade.

The pattern repeats with unnerving regularity. Genuine progress on a narrow problem → over-broad claims to secure funding → enormous investment → the claims come due → a critical report or a market crash → collapse. It happened in the mid-1970s and again in the late 1980s. It is the main reason experienced researchers still flinch when the current wave gets called inevitable.

1966 – 1980

The first winter

1966 · the warning shot

The ALPAC report

The US government had spent roughly $20 million on machine translation, mostly hoping to read Russian scientific literature automatically. A committee of the National Academy of Sciences examined the results and reported that machine translation was slower, less accurate and more expensive than human translators — and that there was no immediate prospect of that changing. Funding for the field was effectively eliminated in the United States for two decades.

1969 · the rules change

The Mansfield Amendment

US Congress required that defence research money go to work with a “direct and apparent relationship” to a military function. Open-ended basic research in AI — which had been generously funded on trust — suddenly had to justify itself against specific deliverables. It largely could not.

1973 · the killing blow

The Lighthill report

The British Science Research Council asked the applied mathematician Sir James Lighthill to assess AI research. His report was devastating and, on the evidence available, largely fair. Its core finding was the combinatorial explosion: techniques that worked on toy problems became hopeless at realistic scale, and nothing in the field’s progress suggested this would change.

Lighthill concluded that in no part of the field had the discoveries so far produced the major impact that had been promised. AI research in Britain was dismantled at all but two or three universities. A televised BBC debate followed, in which Lighthill faced John McCarthy and others; it did not save the funding.

1974 – 1980 · the freeze

DARPA loses patience

DARPA had funded a large Speech Understanding Research programme at Carnegie Mellon, expecting a system that could take spoken commands. What it got worked only on a small vocabulary, spoken slowly, by specific people. The programme was cancelled in 1976. Across the US and UK, graduate students were advised to describe their work as anything other than artificial intelligence.

1980 – 1987

The boom in between: expert systems

AI came back not by getting more ambitious but by getting far less so. The new pitch dropped general intelligence entirely: pick one narrow professional task, extract the rules a human expert uses, and encode them.

These expert systems had a clean two-part architecture. A knowledge base held hundreds or thousands of if–then rules gathered from specialists. An inference engine chained those rules together to reach conclusions, and could explain its reasoning by listing the rules it had used — a feature modern neural networks conspicuously lack.

The systems that worked

  • DENDRAL (Stanford, from 1965) identified molecular structures from mass spectrometry data, matching expert chemists.
  • MYCIN (Stanford, 1972–76) diagnosed blood infections and recommended antibiotics, with about 450 rules. In evaluation it performed comparably to faculty specialists. It was never deployed clinically — partly over liability, and partly because nobody had settled who is responsible when a program is wrong.
  • XCON (Digital Equipment Corporation, 1980) configured VAX computer orders, checking that customers’ requested components would actually work together. By the mid-1980s it was processing tens of thousands of orders and DEC estimated it saved the company around $40 million a year. This was the number every vendor quoted.

An industry formed around it. Companies bought dedicated LISP machines — expensive workstations built specifically to run LISP fast — from Symbolics and Lisp Machines Inc. Governments piled in. Japan launched the Fifth Generation Computer Systems project in 1982 with roughly $850 million, aiming at massively parallel logic-programming machines. Britain answered with the £350 million Alvey programme; the US answered with DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative in 1983. By 1985 corporations were spending over a billion dollars a year on AI.

1987 – 1993

The second winter

The collapse started not with a report but with a market. In 1987 the specialised LISP machine business evaporated in a matter of months. General-purpose workstations from Sun, and even desktop machines from Apple and IBM, had become fast enough to run LISP perfectly well at a fraction of the price. A market worth around half a billion dollars simply stopped existing. Symbolics, once the flagship of commercial AI, never recovered.

Then the systems themselves started disappointing their owners — for four reasons that had been visible all along.

Failure 1

The knowledge acquisition bottleneck

Getting rules out of experts was brutally slow. Specialists could do the job but often could not say how — much of expertise is pattern recognition that never becomes words. A serious system took years of interviews.

Failure 2

Brittleness

Inside its domain, an expert system matched professionals. One step outside, it did not degrade gracefully — it produced confident nonsense, with no sense that it had left the territory it understood.

Failure 3

Maintenance

Knowledge changes. New drugs, new products, new regulations. With thousands of interacting rules, adding one could silently break three others. XCON grew past 10,000 rules and became extremely expensive to keep correct.

Failure 4

They could not learn

An expert system never improved from use. Every ounce of competence had to be installed by a human. That single limitation is what the next twenty years of research reversed.

Japan’s Fifth Generation project ended in 1992 having produced respectable research into parallel logic programming and essentially no commercial impact. DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative was wound down after concluding that AI was not the near-term route to its goals. By 1993, “AI” was a word that made investors leave the room.

How the field survived: it changed its name

The work did not stop. It went underground and renamed itself. Researchers submitted grant applications for machine learning, knowledge-based systems, informatics, pattern recognition, computational intelligence, intelligent agents — anything but AI. Nils Nilsson later described this as the field’s “AI winter clothing”.

Two things happened during the freeze that mattered enormously later:

  • The methods got more mathematical. Cut off from the promise of general intelligence, researchers focused on making narrow systems provably good. Statistics, probability theory and optimisation replaced hand-written logic. This is the intellectual foundation of everything that came after.
  • Neural networks quietly came back. The 1986 publication of backpropagation by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams solved the multi-layer training problem that Perceptrons had left open — but it landed just as the money was leaving, so it took another twenty-five years and a great deal more computing power to show what it could do.

The lesson people actually took

Not “AI is impossible”, but “stop selling general intelligence and start shipping measurable results”. That discipline — benchmarks, error rates, held-out test sets, honest comparison against the previous best — is what made the next thirty years work. It is also, worth noting, the discipline the current boom is under the most pressure to abandon.
