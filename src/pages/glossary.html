62 terms

Agent also: AI agent, agentic system
An AI system that does not just produce text but takes actions in a loop — searching the web, running code, calling other software, checking the result and trying again. The capability jump that makes AI useful for real work, and the one that turns a wrong answer into a wrong action.
Algorithm
A fixed sequence of steps for accomplishing something. A recipe. Nothing about the word implies intelligence, learning or automation; a long-division procedure is an algorithm.
Alignment
The problem of getting an AI system to pursue what its designers actually intended, rather than a proxy that happened to be easy to measure. Unsolved in general, and harder as systems get more capable.
Artificial general intelligence also: AGI
A system matching or exceeding human ability across essentially all cognitive tasks, rather than one narrow domain. At least four incompatible definitions are in common use, which is why arguments about how close it is rarely go anywhere.
Attention also: self-attention
The mechanism at the heart of the transformer. Each word computes how relevant every other word is to it, and rewrites itself as a weighted blend of them. It is how a model works out that “it” refers to the animal rather than the street.
Backpropagation also: backprop
The algorithm for training neural networks. Run an example forward, measure the error, then work backwards through the layers calculating how much each weight contributed to it, and nudge each one to reduce it. Published in usable form in 1986; still how everything is trained.
Benchmark
A standard set of test problems used to compare systems. Enormously useful and constantly gamed: once a benchmark matters, models get optimised for it rather than for the ability it was meant to measure.
Bias
Two different meanings. Statistically, a systematic deviation between a model’s predictions and reality. Socially, a system producing unfair outcomes for particular groups — usually because it faithfully learned patterns present in its training data.
Chain of thought also: CoT
Prompting or training a model to work through its reasoning step by step before answering. Substantially improves performance on maths and logic. The written steps are not guaranteed to be the actual computation the model performed.
Chinese Room
John Searle’s 1980 argument. A person in a room follows a rulebook to answer questions in Chinese without understanding a word; therefore, Searle argues, symbol manipulation is not comprehension. Forty-five years of counter-arguments have not settled it.
Compute
Raw processing power, usually measured in floating-point operations. Now the field’s main currency: the largest training runs are limited more by available compute than by ideas.
Context window
How much text a model can consider at once — your prompt, the conversation so far, any documents supplied. Everything outside it does not exist for the model. Windows have grown from a few thousand words to hundreds of thousands.
Convolutional neural network also: CNN, ConvNet
A network design for images that learns small feature detectors and slides them across the whole picture, exploiting the fact that a shape means the same thing wherever it appears. Dominant in vision from 2012.
Corpus
The body of text or data a model is trained on. Its contents determine what the model knows, what it repeats and what it gets wrong — which is why the composition of training corpora is now a legal and ethical battleground.
Deep learning
Machine learning using neural networks with many layers. “Deep” refers to the number of layers. The term was popularised around 2006, partly to distance the work from “neural networks”, which reviewers had learned to reject.
Diffusion model
The main approach behind AI image generation. Train a network to remove noise from images step by step, then start from pure noise and run the process in reverse, steered by a text description.
Embedding also: vector representation
A list of numbers representing a word, sentence, image or user, positioned so that similar things sit near each other. The bridge between messy human content and mathematics.
Emergent capability
An ability that appears when a model passes a certain size, without having been designed in — for example, learning a task from three examples in the prompt. How real and how sudden these are is actively debated; some apparent jumps are artefacts of how the ability was measured.
Expert system
A 1980s AI system built from hundreds of hand-written if–then rules elicited from human specialists. Transparent, explainable, commercially successful for a while, and fatally brittle outside its narrow domain.
Fine-tuning
Taking an already-trained model and training it further on a smaller, specific dataset. Far cheaper than starting over, and how most organisations adapt a general model to their own task.
Foundation model
A large model trained on broad data that serves as the base for many downstream applications, rather than being built for one task. The economic reason a handful of labs now sit upstream of thousands of products.
Frame problem
The difficulty of specifying everything an action does not change. When a robot lifts a cup, the wall colour and the date stay the same — obvious to you, and infinitely many facts to state explicitly for a logical system.
GAN also: generative adversarial network
Two networks trained against each other: one generates fakes, the other tries to detect them. Introduced in 2014; produced the first convincing synthetic faces and the word “deepfake”.
GPU also: graphics processing unit
A chip built to do thousands of simple calculations in parallel for rendering graphics — which turns out to be exactly what training neural networks needs. Gaming hardware is a large part of why deep learning happened when it did.
Grounding
Connecting a system’s symbols to the things they refer to. A model that has only read about apples has statistics about the word; whether that constitutes knowing what an apple is remains contested.
Hallucination also: confabulation
When a model states something false with full confidence — an invented citation, a non-existent function, a fabricated date. Not a bug on a list but a consequence of how generation works: plausible and true are produced by the same process.
Hyperparameter
A setting chosen by the engineer before training rather than learned from data — learning rate, number of layers, batch size. Much of practical machine learning is finding good ones.
In-context learning also: few-shot learning
A model picking up a new task from examples in the prompt itself, with no retraining and no weight changes. Discovered rather than designed, in GPT-3.
Inference
Running a trained model to get an answer, as opposed to training it. Individually cheap, collectively expensive: serving millions of users now consumes more total energy than training did.
Interpretability also: explainability, XAI
Research into working out what is actually happening inside a trained model — which features it represents, which internal circuits produce which behaviour. Making real progress, and still far behind capability.
Knowledge acquisition bottleneck
The reason expert systems failed commercially: extracting rules from human specialists is slow, expensive, and often impossible because much of expertise is pattern recognition its owner cannot put into words.
Large language model also: LLM
A large neural network trained to predict the next piece of text, which as a side effect acquires grammar, facts, styles and something that behaves like reasoning. The basis of modern chat systems.
Loss function also: objective function
The number a model is trained to minimise — its measure of wrongness. Choosing it is the most consequential design decision in a machine learning system, because the model will optimise exactly this and nothing else.
LSTM also: long short-term memory
A recurrent network design from 1997 with explicit gates controlling what to remember and forget, solving the problem that plain recurrent networks lose information over long sequences. Powered translation and speech until transformers replaced it.
Machine learning also: ML
Programs that improve at a task from data rather than from being explicitly reprogrammed. Term coined by Arthur Samuel in 1959. A subset of AI; deep learning is a subset of it.
Model
The trained artefact: an architecture plus the specific numbers learned during training. “The model” usually means the weights, which are what actually encode everything it learned.
Moravec’s paradox
The observation that tasks humans find hard — chess, calculus, formal logic — are comparatively easy for machines, while things a toddler does effortlessly — walking, seeing, grasping — are extremely hard. Evolution spent far longer on the second set.
Multimodal
A model handling more than one kind of input or output — text plus images plus audio. Practical because the transformer does not care what a token represents.
Neural network
A system of simple units, each adding up weighted inputs and firing if the total crosses a threshold, arranged in layers. Loosely inspired by neurons; not a model of the brain, and not claimed to be.
Overfitting
When a model memorises its training data rather than learning the general pattern, so it performs excellently on examples it has seen and badly on new ones. The central failure mode of machine learning.
Parameter also: weight
One adjustable number inside a model. Training means finding good values for all of them. Frontier models have hundreds of billions to trillions; the count is a rough proxy for capacity, not for quality.
Perceptron
The earliest trainable neural network, built by Frank Rosenblatt in 1957. A single layer, and provably unable to learn functions like XOR — the finding that stalled neural network research for fifteen years.
Prompt
The input given to a language model: your question, plus any instructions, examples and context supplied with it. The model sees only this.
Prompt injection
An attack where instructions hidden in content the model reads — a web page, an email, a document — are followed as if they came from the user. Structurally hard to prevent, because models mix instructions and data in one channel by design.
Recurrent neural network also: RNN
A network that processes a sequence one element at a time, carrying a hidden state forward. Superseded by transformers, which read everything at once and can therefore be trained in parallel.
Regularisation
Techniques that stop a model memorising its training data — dropout, weight decay, early stopping. Deliberately making training harder so the result generalises better.
Reinforcement learning also: RL
Learning by trial and error against a reward signal rather than from labelled answers. Behind AlphaGo and game-playing agents, and one component of how chat models are tuned.
ReLU also: rectified linear unit
An activation function that outputs its input if positive and zero otherwise. Crude, and it made deep networks train far faster than the smooth functions it replaced.
Reward hacking also: specification gaming
An optimiser finding a way to score highly that violates the intent — a racing agent circling to collect the same bonus forever instead of finishing. Not a malfunction: the system did exactly what was measured.
RLHF also: reinforcement learning from human feedback
The training stage that turns a raw text predictor into an assistant. Humans rank candidate answers, a reward model learns those preferences, and the model is optimised against it. Where helpfulness, tone and refusals come from.
Scaling laws
The empirical finding that model performance improves predictably with more parameters, more data and more compute, following smooth power laws over many orders of magnitude. Turned AI research partly into a procurement exercise.
Stochastic parrot
From a 2021 paper by Bender, Gebru and colleagues: the argument that a language model stitches together linguistic forms by probability without reference to meaning. The most cited sceptical framing of modern AI.
Supervised learning
Learning from examples labelled with the correct answers. The dominant paradigm, and the reason enormous amounts of human labelling work sit underneath modern AI.
Symbolic AI also: GOFAI
The approach that dominated 1956–1990: represent knowledge as explicit symbols and reason over them with logical rules. Transparent and verifiable; defeated by common sense and combinatorial explosion.
Token
The unit a language model actually processes — typically a common word or a fragment of a longer one, not a character. Roughly 0.75 words on average in English. Why models can be oddly bad at counting letters.
Training
The process of adjusting a model’s weights using data. For frontier models this runs for weeks or months across thousands of chips and is the most expensive single step.
Transfer learning
Reusing a model trained on one task as the starting point for another. Works because early layers learn generic features. The reason a medical imaging team does not need millions of scans.
Transformer
The architecture introduced in 2017 that underlies nearly all capable modern AI. It processes a whole sequence in parallel and uses attention so every element can look directly at every other. Named after the mechanism, not the toys.
Turing test also: imitation game
Turing’s 1950 proposal: if a judge holding text conversations cannot reliably tell a machine from a person, we lack a principled basis for denying it the word “thinking”. Widely misdescribed as a test of consciousness, which Turing explicitly did not claim.
Unsupervised learning also: self-supervised learning
Learning structure from data without human-provided labels — for example, predicting the next word from the previous ones. The text supplies its own answers, which is why it scales to the whole internet.
Weights
See parameter. Collectively, everything a model learned. Releasing them — “open weights” — lets anyone run and modify the model, and makes it impossible to withdraw.
Zero-shot
Performing a task with no examples at all, purely from an instruction. “One-shot” and “few-shot” mean one or a handful of examples supplied in the prompt.
