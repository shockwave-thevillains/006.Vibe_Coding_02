June 2017

Eight pages that reorganised the field

A paper from Google with the faintly cheeky title “Attention Is All You Need” introduced the transformer. It was aimed at machine translation. It ended up underneath essentially every capable AI system built since — text, images, audio, video, protein structures, robot control.

To see why it mattered, you need the problem it solved. Recurrent networks read a sentence the way you do: one word at a time, carrying a running summary. That has two costs. First, it is sequential — word 500 cannot be processed until words 1 to 499 are done, so you cannot spread the work across thousands of processors. Second, information from early words gets diluted with every step, so long-range connections are weak.

The transformer threw out the sequence. Every word is processed at the same time, and each word looks directly at every other word to decide what is relevant to it. Training that used to take weeks could be done in days, on far more data. The recipe scaled — and scaling turned out to be the whole game.

The mechanism

What “attention” actually does

Take the sentence: “The animal didn’t cross the street because it was too tired.” What does it refer to? You answered instantly. The animal — because streets do not get tired. Change one word to “too wide” and it now means the street.

Nothing in the grammar tells you this. It comes from knowing what tired things and wide things are. Attention is the mechanism that lets a network use that kind of context.

Three roles for every word

For each word, the model computes three vectors, and the standard analogy is a library:

  • A query — what this word is looking for. For “it”: “I am a pronoun; which noun am I standing in for?”
  • A key — what this word offers. For “animal”: “I am a noun, animate, singular.”
  • A value — the actual content to pass along if selected.

Every query is compared against every key. The better the match, the more of that word’s value flows into the current word’s new representation. Do this for all words at once and every word ends up rewritten in terms of the context that matters to it — and the whole operation is one large matrix multiplication, which is exactly what a GPU is built for.

Real models run many of these in parallel — multi-head attention — so different heads can track different relationships: one for grammatical subject, one for what modifies what, one for long-range topic. Stack that block 30, 80, 120 times and you have a modern language model.

One detail that explains a lot

Because attention has no inherent sense of order, position has to be added explicitly as extra numbers on each word. This is why models can be surprisingly clumsy with tasks like counting letters or reversing a list: order is something they are told, not something built into how they read.

2018 – 2020

Two forks in the road, then a surprise

2018 — Google

BERT: read in both directions

BERT was trained by hiding random words and asking the model to fill them in, using context from both sides. That makes it excellent at understanding text — classification, search relevance, question answering — and useless at writing it, since it has no notion of “what comes next”. Google put it into search within a year, calling it the biggest ranking change in half a decade.

2018–19 — OpenAI

GPT: predict the next word

The other fork trained on a single, almost absurdly simple objective: given all previous words, predict the next one. Repeat over a large fraction of the public internet. It sounds like autocomplete, and mechanically it is. But to predict the next word well across arbitrary text you have to learn grammar, facts, styles, arithmetic, and something that behaves like reasoning — because all of those show up in the data.

2019

The release that started an argument

OpenAI initially withheld the full GPT-2 model, citing misuse risk, and released it in stages over nine months. Critics called it hype; others called it the first serious attempt at responsible release. Either way it opened the debate about staged and open release that is still running.

2020 — GPT-3

In-context learning

175 billion parameters, roughly a hundred times its predecessor. The unexpected result was not fluency — it was that you could teach it a new task in the prompt itself. Show three examples of translating slang into formal English and it does the fourth. No retraining, no gradient updates. Nobody had designed this in; it appeared with scale.

Scaling laws

In 2020 OpenAI researchers published something that reads more like physics than computer science: model performance improves as a smooth, predictable power law in three quantities — parameters, training data, and compute. No plateau appeared in the range they could test. You could forecast how good a model would be before building it.

This turned research into procurement. If more compute reliably means more capability, the question stops being “what is the right architecture?” and becomes “how many chips can you buy?” — which is a large part of why AI research shifted from universities to a handful of very well-funded labs.

In 2022 DeepMind’s Chinchilla paper corrected the recipe: the existing giant models were badly under-trained on data relative to their size. A smaller model fed far more text beat a much larger one. The industry re-balanced, and the value of high-quality training data went up sharply — with consequences you can read about in Chapter 07.

2022

The year AI became a consumer product

Two things happened in 2022 that took AI out of research and into daily life.

Images from sentences

Text-to-image generation went from novelty to production in about twelve months. Diffusion models work by an odd route: take a real image, add noise repeatedly until it is static, and train a network to reverse each step. Then start from pure noise and run the reversal, steered by a text description. DALL·E 2, Midjourney and Stable Diffusion all shipped in 2022; Stable Diffusion’s weights were released publicly, so anyone with a decent graphics card could run it. The copyright and consent fights started immediately.

30 November 2022

ChatGPT was, by OpenAI’s own description, a low-key research preview of an instruction-tuned model. It reached an estimated one million users in five days and roughly a hundred million within two months — among the fastest consumer adoption curves ever recorded.

The technical ingredient was RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback). A raw pre-trained model, asked a question, may well continue with more questions — it is completing a document, not answering you. RLHF is how a model is taught what a good answer looks like: humans rank candidate responses, a reward model learns to predict those rankings, and the model is then optimised to score well. Helpfulness, tone, and refusals all come from this stage.

What followed was an arms race in public: GPT-4 in 2023 with image input and far stronger reasoning; Meta’s Llama models released with open weights, seeding a large self-hosted ecosystem; Anthropic’s Claude, Google’s Gemini, Mistral, DeepSeek and others; and context windows growing from a few thousand words to hundreds of thousands.

2023 – now

Thinking longer, and doing things

Reasoning models

The next shift was not a bigger model but a different way of using one. Researchers found that prompting a model to “think step by step” sharply improved its performance on maths and logic — and that models could be trained to do this automatically, generating long internal chains of reasoning before answering, checking their own work and backtracking.

This introduced a second scaling axis. Alongside training compute, there is now inference compute: letting a model spend seconds or minutes on a hard problem instead of milliseconds. Harder question, longer thinking, better answer — and a larger bill.

Multimodal by default

Frontier models now take images, audio and video as input as a matter of course, and generate several of those as output. The reason is structural: the transformer does not care what a token represents. Chop an image into patches, an audio clip into slices, and the same architecture handles all of them in one shared representation.

Agents and tools

A model that only writes text is limited by what it memorised. Give it tools — web search, a code interpreter, a database, an email client — and it can look things up, run and debug its own code, and take multi-step actions. This is the current frontier of deployment, and the current frontier of risk: a system that can only be wrong is a nuisance; a system that can be wrong and then act is something else. Standardised interfaces for connecting models to tools have emerged to make this manageable.

What people assumeWhat is actually true
It looks up answers in a databaseThere is no database. Knowledge is spread across billions of weights and reconstructed on the fly. This is why it cannot cite a source unless it has been given search tools — and why it can produce a plausible, non-existent citation.
It remembers your conversationsBy default each session starts empty. The apparent memory within a conversation is the whole transcript being re-fed to the model on every turn. Persistent memory across sessions is a separate feature built on top.
It knows when it is wrongIt produces the most probable continuation. A confident falsehood and a confident truth are generated by the same process, which is why hallucination is a structural property rather than a bug to be patched.
Bigger is always betterChinchilla showed data matters as much as size, and smaller specialised models routinely beat larger general ones on specific tasks — at a fraction of the cost.
It understands what it saysGenuinely unsettled, and the most interesting open question in the field. It reliably does things that look like understanding, and fails in ways no understanding human would. Both observations are real. See Chapter 09.
