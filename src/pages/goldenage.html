1956 – 1966

Everything worked, so everything seemed possible

The decade after Dartmouth produced a run of results that genuinely deserved the excitement. Computers with less memory than a modern doorbell were doing things no machine had ever done. It is worth taking the optimism seriously before laughing at it — if you had been in the room, you would probably have shared it.

The dominant approach had a name: symbolic AI, sometimes called Good Old-Fashioned AI. The bet was simple and elegant. Intelligence is reasoning. Reasoning is manipulating symbols according to rules. Computers manipulate symbols beautifully. Therefore: write down the right symbols and the right rules, and you get thought.

Six programs that made people believe

1957 — Newell & Simon

General Problem Solver

An attempt at a universal reasoner. GPS was given a starting state and a goal state, and searched for a sequence of moves between them using means–ends analysis: look at the biggest difference between where you are and where you want to be, and pick an action that reduces it.

It solved logic puzzles, the Tower of Hanoi, and word problems. It also revealed the flaw that would define the era: it could not tell which differences mattered, so on any realistic problem the search space exploded.

1958 — John McCarthy

LISP

A programming language built on one idea: code and data have the same shape — nested lists. That means a program can read, build and modify other programs, including itself, as easily as it edits a list of numbers.

For symbolic AI, where the whole job is shuffling symbolic structures, this was the perfect tool. LISP dominated AI research for thirty years and gave the world garbage collection, recursion as a first-class idiom, and the read–eval–print loop.

1959 — Arthur Samuel

The checkers player

Samuel’s IBM 701 program played checkers — and, crucially, improved. It played thousands of games against itself, adjusted how it scored board positions based on which choices led to wins, and remembered positions it had already evaluated.

By 1962 it beat a self-described state champion. Samuel coined the phrase machine learning to describe what it did: getting better at a task without being explicitly reprogrammed for each improvement.

1966 — Joseph Weizenbaum

ELIZA

A pattern-matching script that imitated a Rogerian psychotherapist by turning whatever you typed back into a question. “My mother hates me” became “Tell me more about your family.” It understood nothing. There was no model of the conversation, no memory, no meaning — roughly 200 lines of substitution rules.

People confided in it anyway. Weizenbaum’s own secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it privately. He was so disturbed by how readily people attributed understanding to a trivial program that he spent the rest of his career as one of AI’s sharpest critics.

1966–72 — SRI

Shakey the robot

The first mobile robot that reasoned about its own actions. Shakey could be told “push the block off the platform”, work out that it first needed to find a ramp, plan the route, and execute it — perceiving the room with a TV camera and replanning when things went wrong.

It moved at about two metres per hour and needed a radio link to a mainframe. But the planner built for it, STRIPS, is the direct ancestor of the automated planning systems used today in logistics and spacecraft operations.

1970 — Terry Winograd

SHRDLU

The high-water mark of symbolic AI, and the program that best shows why it stalled. SHRDLU controlled a simulated table of coloured blocks and held real conversations about it: “Find a block which is taller than the one you are holding and put it in the box.” It resolved pronouns, asked for clarification when a request was ambiguous, and could explain why it had done something.

It was genuinely impressive and genuinely a trap. Everything SHRDLU knew was hand-coded about a world of a dozen blocks. Nobody found a way to scale that to a world containing kitchens, weather, sarcasm and other people.

1957 – 1969

The perceptron, and the book that buried it

Running in parallel to symbolic AI was a rival tradition. In 1957 Frank Rosenblatt, a psychologist at Cornell, built the perceptron: a machine that learned to classify patterns without being told the rules.

The Mark I Perceptron was physical hardware. A 20×20 grid of photocells looked at a card; motor-driven potentiometers held the weights; the machine adjusted them itself. Show it letters, tell it when it was wrong, and the weights drifted toward a setting that worked. It was learning in the Hebbian sense — connections that helped got stronger.

The press coverage was extravagant even by modern standards. The US Navy told reporters it expected the perceptron to be “the embryo of an electronic computer that will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence”.

Then, 1969

Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, a careful mathematical study of exactly what a single-layer perceptron can and cannot do. Their central example was XOR — a rule as simple as “one or the other, but not both”.

A single-layer perceptron draws one straight line through the data and calls everything on one side “yes”. For XOR, no straight line works. The proof is airtight, and it generalises: any pattern that is not linearly separable is out of reach.

Minsky and Papert knew that multi-layer networks could represent XOR — they said so. Their point was that nobody knew how to train one. But the nuance did not survive contact with funding committees. Neural network research lost most of its money for fifteen years, and Rosenblatt died in a boating accident in 1971, aged 43, with the field he founded in retreat.

The gap that would be closed

The missing piece was an efficient way to work out how much each weight in a hidden layer contributed to the final error. That algorithm — backpropagation — was published in a usable form in 1986, and the whole field reversed direction. See Chapter 04.

The promises

Predictions that did not survive contact with reality

These were not press-release exaggerations. They were serious statements by the field’s most senior figures, made to funders and in print.

Machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do.

Herbert Simon, 1965

Within a generation … the problem of creating ‘artificial intelligence’ will substantially be solved.

Marvin Minsky, 1967

In 1958 Simon and Newell predicted a computer would be world chess champion within ten years. It took thirty-nine. In 1970 Minsky reportedly told Life magazine that a machine with the general intelligence of an average human was three to eight years away.

Statements like these were made directly to the agencies writing the cheques. When the deadlines passed with nothing delivered, the cheques stopped — and the next chapter happened.

Four walls the golden age ran into

1. Combinatorial explosion

Symbolic AI solves problems by searching through possibilities. The trouble is that possibilities multiply. A chess position has roughly 30 legal moves; look ten moves ahead and you are examining more positions than there are grains of sand on Earth. Programs that breezed through toy problems became unusable the moment the problem got real — not because the computers were slow, but because the numbers grow faster than any hardware ever will.

2. The commonsense problem

To understand “the trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big”, you need to know that trophies are rigid, suitcases have interiors, and “it” here means the trophy — because if it meant the suitcase, the sentence would say “too small”. None of that is in the sentence. It is in you. Writing down everything an ordinary adult knows turned out to be a task with no visible end.

3. The frame problem

When a robot picks up a cup, what changes? The cup’s position, obviously. What does not change? The colour of the wall, the day of the week, the position of every other object in the building, the price of tea. Humans never consider these. A logical system has to be told, explicitly, about every fact that an action leaves alone — and there are infinitely many of those.

4. Micro-worlds do not add up

The strategy was to master a tiny domain and then scale. But SHRDLU’s success came from the smallness of its world, not despite it. There is no ladder from a table of blocks to a kitchen. Each new domain meant starting the hand-coding again.

Worth keeping in mind: none of these problems were ever solved. Modern AI did not defeat the frame problem or write down common sense — it went around them, by learning statistical patterns from enormous amounts of human-produced text and images instead of reasoning from explicit facts. The walls are still there. We built a different road.
