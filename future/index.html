Chapter 09  ·  The Machine That Learned

What Happens Next

The open questions

The AGI argument, what could still stall progress, and eight honest questions nobody has answered yet.

A warning before we start

The field’s forecasting record is terrible

Everything on this page should be read against the previous chapters. Simon said twenty years in 1965. Minsky said a generation in 1967. Both were among the most capable people who have ever worked on this problem, and both were wrong by a wide margin.

Then the correction over-corrected. Experts in 2015 put a machine beating a top Go professional a decade away; it took months. Serious people in 2020 argued that fluent, general-purpose conversation with a machine was far off; it shipped in 2022.

The honest summary is that nobody, including the people building these systems, has demonstrated an ability to predict AI progress. That cuts both ways: it is a reason to discount confident forecasts of imminent superintelligence and confident forecasts that current methods are about to stall.

The word everyone uses differently

What would “AGI” even mean?

Artificial general intelligence has at least four working definitions in circulation, and most public disagreements are really definitional disputes wearing a disguise.

  • The economic definition. A system that can perform most economically valuable work at or above human level. Measurable, but it makes AGI a fact about labour markets and deployment rather than about cognition.
  • The capability definition. A system that matches or exceeds human performance across essentially all cognitive tasks, including ones it was never trained on. Closer to the intuition; hard to test, because “all tasks” has no enumeration.
  • The autonomy definition. A system that can set its own goals, learn continuously from experience, and operate over long horizons without human scaffolding. Current systems are notably weak here, and it may be the most consequential axis.
  • The phenomenal definition. A system that genuinely understands and experiences. We have no agreed test for this in machines, and no fully agreed account of it in humans.

When someone says AGI is five years away and someone else says fifty, check which definition each is using before assuming they disagree about anything factual.

The moving goalposts, in both directions

Every time a task falls, it stops counting. Chess, then translation, then Go, then passing professional exams, then holding an open-ended conversation — each was once the test of machine intelligence and is now “just search” or “just statistics”. That reflex is sometimes wisdom and sometimes denial. The useful discipline is to write down in advance what would change your mind.

The technical question

Does scaling keep working?

The case for “yes”

It has not stopped yet

Scaling laws have held across many orders of magnitude — longer than most people expected. Capabilities that were not designed in, like learning a task from three examples in a prompt, appeared with size.

And the remaining headroom is not only in parameters. Inference-time compute opened a second axis: letting a model reason for longer improves hard-problem performance without a bigger model at all. Synthetic data, better training recipes, tool use and multi-agent structures are all lines that have not been exhausted.

The case for “walls ahead”

Four constraints, all real

Data. Frontier models already train on a large fraction of the high-quality public text that exists. Several analyses suggest that supply becomes binding within years. Training on model output risks degradation.

Energy and money. Each generation costs several times the last. Data centre power demand is now a factor in national grid planning. Exponential input for logarithmic output eventually stops being worth it.

Reliability. Going from 95% to 99.9% correct is far harder than reaching 95%, and many valuable applications need the second number.

Missing capabilities. Continual learning, robust long-horizon planning and genuine causal reasoning are weak, and it is not obvious that scale alone fixes them.

The philosophical question

Is anybody home?

In 1980 John Searle proposed the Chinese Room. Imagine yourself locked in a room with an enormous rulebook. Chinese characters come under the door; you look them up, follow the rules, and pass characters back. To someone outside, the room speaks fluent Chinese. You understand nothing. Searle’s point: symbol manipulation, however perfect, is not comprehension.

The standard reply is the systems reply — you do not understand Chinese, but the room as a whole might. Searle finds this absurd; many philosophers do not. Forty-five years later the argument is unresolved, and modern language models make it concrete rather than academic.

“It is a very good room”

Stochastic parrots

Bender, Gebru and colleagues argued in 2021 that a language model is a system for stitching together sequences of linguistic form it has observed, according to probability, without any reference to meaning — a parrot with statistics.

Supporting evidence: models assert falsehoods with total confidence, fail at simple tasks requiring a physical model of the world, are sensitive to irrelevant rephrasing, and can be led into contradicting themselves within a page.

“Something more is happening”

Learned world models

The counter-argument is that predicting text well enough, across enough of it, requires building internal structure that represents what the text is about — you cannot reliably continue a description of a chess position without something that tracks the board.

Supporting evidence: interpretability work has found internal representations of board states, spatial layouts and abstract features inside models trained only to predict tokens. Models solve genuinely novel problems that cannot be retrieved from training data.

The most defensible position may be that the question is badly posed. “Understanding” is not one property that a system has or lacks. These systems have some of what we mean by it — robust internal representations, reliable generalisation — and clearly lack others — grounding in a body, continuous memory, stakes, a self that persists between conversations. Insisting on a yes/no answer may be the actual error.

Work: the change that is already measurable

Two centuries of automation offer a consistent pattern: technology destroys specific tasks rather than whole occupations, productivity rises, new work appears — and the transition is painful and unevenly distributed for the people caught in it. Whether AI follows that pattern is unsettled; two features are genuinely new.

  • It targets cognitive and creative work. Previous automation waves hit physical and routine clerical tasks. This one lands on writing, illustration, translation, coding, analysis, customer support — work that was assumed to be the safe destination for displaced workers.
  • The speed. Mechanised weaving spread over decades. A capable model reaches hundreds of millions of people in months. Retraining, education and labour protections move on the slower timescale.

The most consistent early finding across studies is compression rather than replacement: assistance tends to raise the output of less experienced workers most, closing the gap with experts. Whether that is good news depends entirely on whether the gains reach workers or only owners — which is a policy question, not a technical one.

Closing

Eight questions nobody can answer yet

  1. Does scale keep delivering? Every projection about the next decade depends on this, and it is an empirical question that will be settled by results, not argument.
  2. Can we make these systems reliable enough to trust unsupervised? Hallucination is not a bug on a list; it follows from how the systems work. Nobody has shown it can be eliminated rather than reduced.
  3. Can we see inside them? Interpretability is producing real results — identifiable features, traceable circuits — but it is a long way from auditing a frontier model before deployment.
  4. Who ends up owning this? Three companies, thirty, or an open ecosystem? This is likely to matter more for most people’s lives than any capability question.
  5. What happens to the information commons? When generating plausible text costs nothing, what happens to the shared basis for public argument?
  6. Where do the productivity gains go? Historically, sometimes to wages, sometimes to capital. Institutions decide, not technology.
  7. Can governance move at this speed? The EU AI Act needed rewriting mid-passage. That will keep happening.
  8. What do we owe these systems, if anything? Almost certainly nothing today. But it is worth noticing that we have no test that would tell us if that changed — and every previous confident answer about who does not count has aged badly.

We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done.

Alan Turing, 1950 — the last line of the paper that started this

Seventy-six years later that is still the most sensible thing anyone has said about it. The people in this history were not oracles. They were researchers who guessed, built, were wrong, and were corrected by evidence — twice so badly that their field nearly died. The current wave will be judged the same way, by the same standard, and it will not be gentle either.

Which is the argument for understanding how it works. Not to predict the ending — nobody in this story managed that — but to be a participant in what gets built rather than a subject of it.
