The People Behind the Machines

Twelve lives

Turing, McCarthy, Minsky, Rosenblatt, Weizenbaum, Pearl, Hinton, LeCun, Bengio, Li, Hassabis and Gebru: who built the field, and who argued with them.

Twelve lives

The builders, and the people who argued with them

A field is not a list of results. It is a set of people who disagreed with each other in public for seventy years. Several of these twelve are still disagreeing — sometimes with their own earlier positions.

Alan Turing

1912 – 1954 · The question

Turing did not build an AI. He built the reason to think one was possible — twice.

In 1936 he defined computation itself, proving that a single universal machine could imitate any other. In 1950 he asked whether such a machine could think, decided the question was unanswerable as posed, and replaced it with a test anyone could run. Both moves converted philosophy into engineering.

During the war he led the team at Bletchley Park that broke the German naval Enigma, work credited with shortening the conflict substantially and kept secret for decades. In 1952 he was prosecuted for homosexuality and sentenced to chemical castration. He died in 1954, aged 41. Britain apologised in 2009 and pardoned him in 2013.

John McCarthy

1927 – 2011 · The name and the language

McCarthy named the field, built its language, and spent fifty years insisting it should be built on logic.

He coined “artificial intelligence” in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal — partly because it was vague enough that nobody could argue their work did not qualify. In 1958 he designed LISP, in which programs and data share one structure, so a program can manipulate programs. He also invented garbage collection and pioneered time-sharing, the idea that made interactive computing possible.

He argued to the end that intelligence requires explicit, inspectable representations of facts and the ability to reason over them — the position the statistical revolution ran over. Increasingly, people building agents that must be verified are re-reading him.

Marvin Minsky

1927 – 2016 · The critic inside

Minsky co-founded the MIT AI Lab, built the first neural network machine in 1951, and then wrote the book that shut neural network research down for fifteen years.

Perceptrons (1969, with Seymour Papert) was rigorous and, on its own terms, correct: a single-layer perceptron cannot represent XOR. The authors knew multi-layer networks could; nobody yet knew how to train one. Funding bodies took the simpler message.

His later work — frames, and The Society of Mind, which argues that intelligence is not one mechanism but many simple agents interacting — remains influential. He is the field’s clearest example of how a legitimate technical criticism, delivered at the wrong moment, can redirect a discipline for a generation.

Frank Rosenblatt

1928 – 1971 · The first learner

Rosenblatt built the first machine that learned from its own mistakes rather than being programmed.

The Mark I Perceptron was physical: photocells for eyes, motorised potentiometers for weights. Show it examples, tell it when it was wrong, and it adjusted itself toward getting them right. That is the entire principle of modern machine learning, running in 1958 hardware.

He was also the field’s first cautionary tale about publicity. The press coverage promised a machine that would walk, talk and be conscious of its own existence. When Perceptrons punctured the claims, the correction was as excessive as the hype. He drowned in a boating accident in 1971, aged 43, and did not live to see his approach win.

Joseph Weizenbaum

1923 – 2008 · The conscience

Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA in 1966 to demonstrate how shallow machine conversation was. It did the opposite.

The program had no memory, no model of meaning, and roughly 200 substitution rules. People poured out their private lives to it anyway. His own secretary, who had watched him build it, asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it alone.

That reaction changed his career. In Computer Power and Human Reason (1976) he argued that the real danger was not machines becoming intelligent but humans deciding that whatever machines can do is all that thinking amounts to — and handing over judgements, especially about other people, that require responsibility rather than computation. Every chatbot conversation since has been an experiment in the effect that carries his name.

Judea Pearl

b. 1936 · Uncertainty and cause

Pearl gave AI two things logic could not provide: a rigorous way to reason about uncertainty, and later, a mathematics of causation.

Bayesian networks (1988) let a system hold beliefs with degrees of confidence and update all of them correctly when one new fact arrives. That made expert reasoning possible in medicine, diagnostics and risk without pretending the world was certain.

His later work is arguably more radical. Statistics can tell you that two things occur together; it cannot, by itself, tell you that one causes the other, or what would have happened otherwise. Pearl built the formal language for those questions. His critique of modern machine learning is precise: systems trained purely on correlation have no principled way to answer “what if we changed this?” — and most decisions people care about are exactly that question.

Geoffrey Hinton

b. 1947 · Persistence, then alarm

Hinton spent roughly thirty years working on an approach most of his field considered discredited, and was right.

He co-authored the 1986 backpropagation paper, kept neural network research alive through the years when it could not get funded, coined “deep learning” in 2006 partly as camouflage, and supervised the students who built AlexNet in 2012. He shared the 2018 Turing Award and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics.

In May 2023 he left Google specifically so he could speak about risk without it reflecting on his employer, saying he had changed his mind about how far away powerful systems were and now regretted parts of his life’s work. Whatever you make of the argument, it is unusual: the person most responsible for a technology publicly revising his estimate of its danger, in the direction that costs him something.

Yann LeCun

b. 1960 · Vision, and dissent

LeCun worked out how to make neural networks see, then argued for years that seeing was not enough.

His convolutional networks, developed from 1989, exploit a simple insight: a shape means the same thing wherever it appears in an image, so learn the detector once and slide it. By the late 1990s his systems were reading a large fraction of American bank cheques. In 2012 the same architecture, scaled up, ended classical computer vision.

He shared the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and Bengio, and has been the most prominent sceptic of the current direction. His argument: predicting the next token cannot produce a system that understands the physical world, because text does not contain most of what a child learns by four. He advocates architectures that learn predictive world models from observation instead. He is also the loudest defender of open-weight release — putting him in direct opposition to Hinton on risk.

Yoshua Bengio

b. 1964 · Language, and precaution

Bengio’s work made language a tractable target for neural networks, and he has since become one of the most careful public voices on governing them.

His neural language models from the early 2000s introduced learned word representations — the ancestor of the embeddings underneath every modern system. He co-developed attention mechanisms for translation in 2015, two years before the transformer put attention at the centre of everything.

He shares the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and LeCun. Since 2023 he has chaired international scientific assessment of AI risk and argued for a precautionary approach — that when a technology’s worst case is severe and its behaviour cannot be verified, the burden of proof belongs to those deploying it. He arrives at nearly the opposite conclusion from LeCun on almost identical technical knowledge, which is worth sitting with.

Fei-Fei Li

b. 1976 · The data nobody wanted to build

Li’s contribution was not an algorithm. It was noticing that the field was optimising the wrong thing.

In the mid-2000s everyone was refining models on small datasets. Li argued the bottleneck was data: children see an enormous number of examples, and no algorithm could compensate for a training set of a few thousand images. Building ImageNet — 14 million images, hand-labelled through crowdsourcing across 22,000 categories — was considered a strange use of a researcher’s time. Funding was hard to get.

The 2012 result that started the deep learning era was only possible because ImageNet existed. She has since focused on human-centred AI and on getting non-technical voices — clinicians, policymakers, ethicists — into design decisions rather than review meetings after the fact.

Demis Hassabis

b. 1976 · Games as a laboratory

Hassabis was a chess prodigy, then a games designer, then a neuroscientist, and then combined all three.

DeepMind, which he co-founded in 2010, treated games as controlled environments for studying general learning: DQN learning 49 Atari titles from raw pixels with one architecture, then AlphaGo beating Lee Sedol in 2016, then AlphaGo Zero reaching superhuman play from the rules alone.

The payoff came when the same machinery was pointed at science. AlphaFold 2 predicted protein structures at accuracy comparable to experimental methods, addressing a problem open for fifty years, and its predictions were released freely for hundreds of millions of proteins. He shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with John Jumper and David Baker — the strongest existing answer to “what is any of this actually for?”

Timnit Gebru

b. 1982 · Measuring who gets hurt

Gebru’s method is unglamorous and hard to argue with: take the system, measure it separately for each group of people, and publish the numbers.

Gender Shades (2018, with Joy Buolamwini) tested three shipping commercial products. Error rates for lighter-skinned men were under 1%; for darker-skinned women they reached roughly 35%. Nobody had checked, because average accuracy looked acceptable. Several vendors changed their systems; some cities restricted the technology.

She co-authored “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots” in 2020, warning about environmental cost, the impossibility of auditing web-scale training data, and encoded bias. Her departure from Google over the paper became one of the field’s defining disputes about whether industry can host its own critics. She now runs an independent research institute, and her central argument stands: a system that has not been evaluated separately for the people it will affect has not been evaluated.
