The Full Timeline

1936 – today

Every major moment in the history of artificial intelligence on one filterable timeline.

How to use this

Ninety years on one page

Filter by era, or scroll the whole thing. Each entry links back to the chapter that explains it properly — this page is the index, not the argument.

 

1936

Turing describes the universal machine

In On Computable Numbers, Alan Turing defines an abstract device — a tape, a head, a rule table — and proves that one universal version of it can imitate any other. Software, as a concept, starts here.

1943

The artificial neuron

Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts model a brain cell as a threshold logic unit and show that networks of them can compute any logical function. Every neural network since is a descendant.

1945

The stored-program computer

John von Neumann’s report on the EDVAC sets out the architecture — instructions and data in the same memory — that essentially all computers still use.

1948

Information theory and cybernetics

Claude Shannon gives information a unit and a mathematics. Norbert Wiener publishes Cybernetics, making feedback and goal-seeking behaviour an engineering subject.

1949

Hebb’s learning rule

Donald Hebb proposes that connections between neurons that fire together grow stronger — the first physical account of how experience could reshape a network.

1950

“Computing Machinery and Intelligence”

Turing replaces “can machines think?” with the imitation game, answers nine objections in advance, and predicts machines will hold convincing conversations by 2000. Shannon separately publishes the first paper on computer chess.

1951

SNARC, the first neural network machine

Marvin Minsky and Dean Edmonds build a 40-neuron learning machine from vacuum tubes and surplus bomber parts. It learns to solve a maze.

1952

Machines start playing games

Christopher Strachey’s draughts program runs on the Ferranti Mark 1 — among the first working game-playing programs. Arthur Samuel begins his checkers work at IBM.

1955

Logic Theorist

Newell, Simon and Shaw write a program that proves theorems from Principia Mathematica, finding one proof more elegant than the published version.

1956

Dartmouth: the field is named

John McCarthy coins “artificial intelligence” for an eight-week summer workshop. Nothing is concluded, but the attendees go on to run nearly every major AI lab for twenty years.

1957

The perceptron

Frank Rosenblatt builds a machine that learns to classify patterns by adjusting its own weights. Newell and Simon release the General Problem Solver the same year.

1958

LISP

McCarthy designs a language in which code and data have the same structure, so programs can write and modify programs. It dominates AI research for three decades.

1959

“Machine learning” gets its name

Arthur Samuel’s self-improving checkers program prompts the term. McCarthy and Minsky found the MIT AI Project.

1961

Robots reach the factory floor

Unimate, the first industrial robot, starts work at a General Motors plant — automation without intelligence, and commercially transformative.

1965

Expert systems begin; predictions escalate

Work starts on DENDRAL, which identifies molecular structures. Herbert Simon predicts machines will do any work a man can do within twenty years.

1966

ELIZA, and the first funding collapse

Joseph Weizenbaum’s pattern-matching chatbot convinces users it understands them. The ALPAC report finds machine translation not worth funding, and US support for it ends.

1969

Perceptrons, and Shakey

Minsky and Papert prove single-layer perceptrons cannot learn XOR; neural network funding largely disappears for fifteen years. SRI’s Shakey becomes the first robot to reason about its own actions.

1970

SHRDLU

Terry Winograd’s program holds genuine conversations about a world of coloured blocks — the high point of symbolic AI, and a demonstration of why hand-coded micro-worlds do not scale.

1972

MYCIN and Prolog

MYCIN begins at Stanford, diagnosing blood infections at roughly specialist level. Alain Colmerauer’s Prolog makes logic programming practical.

1973

The Lighthill report

Sir James Lighthill reports to the UK government that AI has failed to deliver on its promises, citing the combinatorial explosion. British AI research is dismantled at all but a few universities.

1974

The first winter sets in

DARPA funding tightens sharply. Paul Werbos describes backpropagation in his doctoral thesis; almost nobody notices.

1976

Speech understanding cancelled

DARPA ends its Speech Understanding Research programme after the delivered systems work only on small vocabularies, slowly, for specific speakers.

1980

Expert systems go commercial

Digital Equipment Corporation deploys XCON to configure computer orders; the company later estimates savings of about $40 million a year. The AI industry is reborn as a product business.

1982

National programmes and Hopfield nets

Japan launches the $850 million Fifth Generation project; the UK answers with Alvey. John Hopfield’s work on recurrent networks quietly revives interest in neural models.

1984

Cyc, and a word of warning

Doug Lenat begins Cyc, a decades-long attempt to hand-encode human common sense. At the same AAAI meeting, researchers publicly use the phrase “AI winter” to warn of what is coming.

1986

Backpropagation goes mainstream

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams publish in Nature the method for training multi-layer networks. It solves the problem Perceptrons exposed — and arrives just as the money leaves.

1987

The second winter begins

The specialised LISP machine market collapses within months as general workstations become fast enough and far cheaper. Around half a billion dollars of industry evaporates.

1988

Reasoning under uncertainty

Judea Pearl’s Bayesian networks give AI a rigorous way to handle uncertain knowledge — and later, a mathematics of causation.

1989

Convolutional networks read handwriting

Yann LeCun’s LeNet learns to recognise handwritten digits. Descendants of it will soon be reading a large share of American cheques.

1992

TD-Gammon

Gerald Tesauro’s backgammon program reaches near-world-class strength by playing itself — an early proof that reinforcement learning plus self-play works.

1993

The freeze ends quietly

Japan’s Fifth Generation project concludes with little commercial impact. The field survives by renaming itself: machine learning, informatics, intelligent systems.

1995

Support vector machines and boosting

Cortes and Vapnik publish SVMs; Freund and Schapire publish AdaBoost. Both outperform neural networks on most benchmarks for the next decade.

1997

Deep Blue and LSTM

IBM’s Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov 3½–2½. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber publish LSTM, which solves the forgetting problem in recurrent networks and is then largely ignored for a decade.

2002

AI in the living room

The Roomba becomes the first genuinely mass-market robot — deliberately simple, and commercially successful where cleverer machines were not.

2005

A car drives itself across a desert

Stanford’s Stanley completes the 212 km DARPA Grand Challenge course after no vehicle finished the previous year. The autonomous vehicle industry starts here.

2006

“Deep learning”

Geoffrey Hinton shows deep networks can be trained layer by layer, and deliberately adopts a new name for an old idea that reviewers had learned to reject.

2009

ImageNet

Fei-Fei Li’s team publishes a crowdsourced dataset of over 14 million labelled images. It was an unfashionable project. It turns out to be the missing ingredient.

2011

Watson wins Jeopardy!; Siri ships

IBM’s Watson beats the game show’s champions using massively parallel statistical text analysis. Apple puts speech recognition in everyone’s pocket.

2012

AlexNet

Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton win ImageNet with a deep convolutional network trained on two gaming GPUs, cutting the error rate from 26.2% to 15.3%. Within eighteen months, hand-engineered computer vision is over.

2013

word2vec

Words become vectors whose geometry encodes meaning and analogy, learned purely from co-occurrence. It also inherits every bias present in the text.

2014

GANs and sequence-to-sequence

Ian Goodfellow’s adversarial networks pit a forger against a detective and produce the first convincing synthetic images. Encoder–decoder models reframe translation as pure learning. Google acquires DeepMind.

2015

ResNet, and learning from pixels

Residual connections make networks of 150+ layers trainable and push ImageNet error below typical human rates. DeepMind’s DQN learns 49 Atari games from raw screen pixels. OpenAI is founded.

2016

AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol

DeepMind’s system wins 4–1 in a match watched by over 200 million people. Move 37 in game two is a play no human would make, and it wins.

2017

“Attention Is All You Need”

Eight pages from Google introduce the transformer, which processes every word in parallel and lets each one attend directly to all the others. AlphaGo Zero surpasses its predecessor learning from self-play alone.

2018

BERT and GPT

Two forks of the transformer: BERT reads in both directions and transforms search; GPT predicts the next token and turns out to scale further than anyone expected. Hinton, LeCun and Bengio share the Turing Award.

2019

A staged release, and an argument

OpenAI withholds the full GPT-2 model citing misuse risk, releasing it over nine months. The debate about open versus controlled release begins in earnest.

2020

GPT-3, scaling laws, AlphaFold 2

175 billion parameters produce an unplanned capability: learning a new task from examples in the prompt. Scaling laws show performance improving predictably with compute. AlphaFold 2 effectively solves protein structure prediction.

2021

Images from text, and a warning shot

CLIP and DALL·E connect language to pictures. Bender, Gebru and colleagues publish “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots”; the resulting dispute becomes one of the field’s defining controversies.

2022

Chinchilla, diffusion, and ChatGPT

DeepMind shows existing giant models were badly under-trained on data. Stable Diffusion ships with open weights. On 30 November, ChatGPT is released as a research preview and reaches an estimated 100 million users within two months.

2023

GPT-4, open weights, and governments

Multimodal frontier models arrive; Meta releases Llama 2 with open weights. Hundreds of researchers sign a statement putting AI extinction risk alongside pandemics and nuclear war. The first international AI safety summit is held at Bletchley Park.

2024

The EU AI Act, and two Nobel prizes

The world’s first comprehensive AI law is adopted, regulating applications by risk tier. The Nobel Prize in Physics goes to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for neural network foundations; the Chemistry prize goes in part to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for AlphaFold.

2025

Reasoning, agents and enforcement

Models trained to think in long internal chains before answering become standard, opening a second scaling axis at inference time. Systems that use tools and take multi-step actions move into production — and prompt injection becomes the defining security problem. The EU AI Act’s first prohibitions take effect.

2026

Where the story reaches you

Multimodal, tool-using, long-context models are ordinary infrastructure. Whether this is a plateau, a shoulder, or the middle of a climb is exactly the thing nobody in this timeline has ever successfully predicted.
