A History of Artificial Intelligence

1936 – today

A complete, plain-English history of artificial intelligence: the people, the breakthroughs, the failures and the open questions.

The short version

Seventy years of trying to build a mind

In 1950 a British mathematician asked a question that sounded almost rude: can machines think? He could not answer it, so he did something cleverer — he replaced it with a question you could actually test. That swap started everything you are reading about here.

What followed was not a smooth climb. Artificial intelligence has boomed and collapsed at least three times. Ideas that were declared dead came back and won. Methods that looked unbeatable turned out to be dead ends. Twice, the money vanished almost overnight and a generation of researchers had to rename their own field to keep getting funded.

This site walks the whole road in plain English: what each idea actually was, why smart people believed it, what broke, and what we ended up with. No equations are required. Nothing is skipped because it is inconvenient.

The thread

Three arguments that never went away

1. Symbols or statistics?

Should a thinking machine manipulate meaningful symbols, the way a logician does? Or should it adjust millions of numbers until its output looks right, the way a nervous system seems to? This fight started in the 1950s and it is still going. Today’s systems are overwhelmingly statistical — but they are worst at exactly the reliable, checkable reasoning symbolic AI was good at.

2. Knowledge or learning?

Do you hand the machine what humanity already knows, or do you build something that works it out from examples? The 1980s bet everything on hand-built knowledge and lost. The 2010s bet everything on learning from data and won — at the cost of systems nobody can fully inspect.

3. Is behaviour enough?

Turing’s trick was to stop asking whether a machine really thinks and start asking whether it behaves as though it does. That move made progress possible. It also postponed a question that modern chatbots have made urgent again: if a system produces every outward sign of understanding, what exactly is missing — and how would we tell?
